Plain-English proposal

Read this first

Transparent prices first. Coverage second. Accounts later.

The U.S. health system is expensive because prices, coverage, and payment rules are spread across many programs. This proposal starts with a simpler order: make prices visible and predictable, then move people into automatic protection, income-based support, and a single account system.

Current system

Today the U.S. pays through a maze of programs and prices.

Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, ACA subsidies, employer insurance, private insurance, payroll taxes, premiums, deductibles, and direct out-of-pocket bills all play a role. That fragmentation makes it hard to see what care costs before people need it, and it makes the system harder to explain to the average household.

The result is not just high spending. It is a system where many payers, many eligibility gates, and many hidden prices all overlap at once.

  • Public programs

    Medicare, Medicaid, and CHIP cover large parts of the population, but under different rules.

  • Employer and private coverage

    Work-based insurance and private plans add another layer of premiums and plan complexity.

  • Household spending

    Families still face deductibles, copays, and direct medical bills even when they are insured.

Seven-part system

The replacement system is built around one account and one protection layer.

The proposal does not try to keep every current program intact. It replaces the maze with a simpler sequence that combines savings, insurance, subsidies, prices, and a fallback safety net.

  1. Mandatory savings

    Routine care is prefunded instead of being paid through unpredictable bills.

  2. Universal catastrophic insurance

    Large medical events are covered automatically so families are protected from the worst costs.

  3. Automatic income-based subsidies

    Support follows income and household status instead of requiring repeated applications.

  4. Transparent national prices

    Providers that accept federal money use published prices so accounts do not get drained by hidden inflation.

  5. Safety net after the first layers

    Last-resort help remains for people whose account, insurance, and subsidies still are not enough.

  6. Private insurance as an optional supplement

    Private plans remain legal, but they become upgrades instead of the core way care is paid for.

  7. One automated account system

    A single payment rail handles contributions, benefits, and support without forcing people to juggle separate systems.

System parts, defined

Mandatory savings
Households set aside routine-care money in a structured account instead of depending on unpredictable out-of-pocket bills.
Catastrophic insurance
The system covers large medical events so one serious illness does not erase household finances.
Income-based subsidies
Support rises and falls with household income so help is automatic instead of tied to repeated enrollment checks.
National prices
Providers that accept federal money use transparent reference prices so medical billing is less fragmented and less inflated.
Safety net
Last-resort support remains available when account balances, insurance, and subsidies still do not cover the need.
Supplemental private insurance
Private plans remain legal, but they act as optional upgrades rather than the core way care is financed.
Automated account system
One payment rail manages contributions, claims, and support so people do not need to navigate separate programs.

Reference point

Why Singapore is mentioned

Singapore is used as a structural reference, not a direct copy. The U.S. version would need different rules because the U.S. is larger, older, more federalized, and already has Medicare, Medicaid, employer insurance, and private insurance commitments.

The comparison is here because Singapore shows a system where price discipline and simple financing can coexist. It is useful as a reference for structure, not as a one-to-one policy template.

In the dashboard, Singapore appears only as a benchmark for health spending as a share of GDP. That comparison is meant to frame the argument, not to imply that the United States can copy the same rules without changing them.

  • Not a direct copy

    The comparison is structural, not a blueprint.

  • Different scale

    The U.S. is much larger and more diverse regionally.

  • Different commitments

    Existing Medicare, Medicaid, employer insurance, and private insurance have to be accounted for.

Why prices come first

The first reform is not mandatory savings. The first reform is transparent national prices.

If the U.S. creates savings accounts before hospital and drug prices are controlled, those accounts will be drained by the same inflated prices that make the current system expensive. Price control is the foundation.

Once prices are predictable, universal catastrophic coverage and automatic savings become workable. That order matters more than the final packaging.

Core rule

Any provider accepting federal healthcare money should accept national reference prices for covered services.

Implementation timeline

The transition should move in order, not all at once.

  1. Transparent national prices

    Set the price foundation before asking households to use accounts.

  2. Automated payment and eligibility rail

    Build the IDs, ledger, income feed, claims clearing, and audit system first.

  3. Universal catastrophic insurance

    Protect people from major costs before routine spending shifts into accounts.

  4. Automatic income-based subsidies

    Replace enrollment churn with automatic top-ups and premium support.

  5. Mandatory health savings

    Require savings only after prices and catastrophic coverage are already in place.

  6. Medicaid and ACA conversion

    Convert existing support into top-ups, premium help, and safety-net support over time.

  7. Private insurance as supplemental coverage

    Move private insurance from core coverage to optional upgrades.

  8. Retirement reform later

    Do not bundle full Social Security replacement into the first health bill.

What this does not do

The proposal narrows the financing problem; it does not claim to solve every healthcare issue at once.

  • It does not make healthcare free

    People still contribute through taxes, premiums, savings, or account funding.

  • It does not immediately replace every program

    The transition happens in phases so existing coverage can be converted instead of dropped overnight.

  • It does not eliminate private insurance

    Private coverage remains available, but it moves from the core system to an optional supplement.

  • It does not provide an official federal score

    The figures on this page are explanatory model outputs, not a Congressional Budget Office score.

Final summary

The reform sequence is short enough to state plainly.

Prices -> payment rail -> catastrophic protection -> subsidies -> accounts -> program conversion
